From manual map edits to a real-time event platform

Short-lived traffic events and commercial content raised the same five product questions. I built one publishing path with different approval rules for each use.

The problem

Two unrelated demands, one publishing bottleneck

Traffic operations needed to publish closures, restrictions and temporary controls that lose their value within hours. At the same time a free navigation product needed revenue, and clients wanted to reach drivers before, during and after a drive.

Both ran through the same path: an email request, two approvals, and a data team editing the base map by hand. Removal meant repeating all of it. Turnaround was typically one to two days, so an event could expire before it was published, and a manual edit could attach content to the wrong road.

Speed was the visible problem. The deeper limit was expressiveness: an event could attach to a whole road, but not to a segment, an area, a planned route, a point of interest or an audience. Automating that workflow alone would have produced the same coarse events, only faster.

What the old model could express

One whole road, all users, no end time.

What the business needed to express

Combine a road segment, a drawn area, a planned route, a POI, an audience and a time window, rather than choose from a fixed list.

Product model

Five dimensions, recombined instead of rebuilt

I split the capability along two axes: where past requests had actually varied, and which levers a commercial campaign would plausibly need before any client asked for them. The same five dimensions produced a public-safety notice and a paid campaign. Only the approval path and the delivery rules differed.

The hard part

The real question was how far to abstract

Building per client would have been fast once and unscalable after. A fully generic rule engine would have been flexible in a way operations could not use and delivery could not carry. The composable middle only works if the right dimensions are chosen, and guessing wrong wastes the work.

Deliberately not built

A priority and collision engine

High-priority events could have pre-empted low-priority ones automatically. The more complex the rules, the less a client could predict whether their campaign would actually run.

Predictability first
What we did instead

Manual separation

Operations separated campaigns by time slot and by area. This was manual but predictable for the client. In early monetisation, that predictability mattered more than optimal allocation.

Time and area
What the model missed

Broadcast capacity

A client wanted a longer message in the idle stretch of a drive. Many routes had no gap long enough to play it. Format was modelled as a choice, when it is also a capacity that changes with every route.

A missing dimension
How it was resolved

Recombination, not new code

Shorter copy, moved to the pre- and post-navigation touchpoints, with stronger map display carrying the message. No new feature was built; the fix came from dimensions the model already had.

The model absorbed it

Results and attribution

What the platform proved, and what it did not

Publishing went from one to two days of coordination to seconds after approval. Two business lines ran on one path, with traffic events taking one approval and commercial content two. Throughput settled at roughly 17 commercial placements and about five traffic events a month.

Clients bought the ability to publish into navigation moments; the platform itself was never sold externally. Revenue was received and recognised, not booked or forecast.

Delivery, not conversion: the platform counted users reached, voice plays and map displays, with per-user and peak exposure alongside them. It had no click, footfall or conversion funnel, so it could show that a message was delivered but not that it changed behaviour.
Scale context: this ran inside a navigation product with over 100 million daily active users. That is the environment, not the reach of any single event.

What I contributed

Governance carried the difference, not code

I owned the platform end to end: the product model, the requirement decomposition across seven teams and about thirty engineers, and delivery through to launch. Per-user and peak exposure were first-class metrics from the start, because monetising a navigation moment is also a way to spend a user’s attention.

What I would design differently

Treat broadcast capacity as a dimension in the model, not a constant. And run a real test pass before approval: publish to a designated test audience first, so operations sees the actual result instead of imagining it. Both are how I would build it today, not capabilities that shipped at the time.
